Amy Edmondson's research on hospital teams found something counterintuitive: the best-performing teams reported _more_ errors, not fewer, because they were able to discuss mistakes openly.\[^1] The quiet ones weren't the healthy ones. That finding matters for a much smaller setting than a hospital ward: a team meeting that used to have real argument and now just gets nods. It's a pattern worth checking, not proof you've earned respect and not automatically proof something's wrong, and it has a documented mechanism behind it.

Start by ruling out the wrong worry. Micromanagement is about control over _how_ work gets done: reviewing every line, dictating method. What's described here is different: a team going silent on judgement calls, the moments where they'd normally tell you an approach is risky or a plan has a hole in it. You can leave people entirely alone on execution and still have trained them not to argue with your decisions.

Many new managers, promoted from individual contributor roles, are used to being the person with the answer. That instinct doesn't disappear with the title, and it shows up as a pull toward certainty over inquiry: deciding fast and expecting the team to fall in line, rather than asking what might be wrong with the plan.

An objection is information about a risk you can't see from your seat. Someone on the team is closer to the code, the customer, or the failure mode than you are. When they raise a concern, they're handing you visibility. When they stop, the risk hasn't gone away; it has just moved out of view. Irving Janis, describing groupthink, called this the illusion of unanimity: people mistake silence for agreement, when often it just means no one wanted to be the one to break it.\[^2]

The cost isn't only hidden risk. A manager who treats objections as obstacles to be managed, rather than information to weigh, is read as someone whose judgement can't be questioned, and that's precisely what people stop trusting. Research has found that the large majority of employees have at some point withheld important information from their manager because they feared the consequences of speaking up.\[^3] Going quiet in a meeting is rarely a one-off. It is what people do when they have stopped trusting that raising a concern is worth it.

Three habits help:

- **Ask for the strongest objection first**, before asking for agreement. A genuine question sounds like 'what should we weigh between A and B?', not 'don't you think A's right?' dressed up as a question.
- **Say out loud whether you're gathering input or already deciding.** People calibrate how hard to push back based on which one it is, and guessing wrong trains them to go quiet either way.
- **Treat fast unanimity on a hard call as a signal to dig, not a relief.** Difficult decisions rarely have obvious answers. If everyone agreed instantly, someone probably held back.

Certainty without input is a blind spot, not strength. The way to tell the difference is whether your team still argues with you when it matters.
